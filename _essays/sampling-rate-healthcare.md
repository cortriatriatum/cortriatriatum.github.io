original: |
  When I was first introduced to the concept of sampling rates, I was too anxious about getting through signal processing class to expand its implications in other contexts. Sampling is the process of discretely (separately and individually) recording a continuous event or signal at multiple time points i.e. breaking up a long continuous signal into multiple individual recordings. At an appropriate sampling rate, although there is a limit, the continuous signal could be recreated.

  I think of flip books – individually drawn pictures sequenced to produce a moving animation. In fact, recorded videos are simply individual pictures sequenced at an appropriate frame rate, that is, individual pictures shown in a fast enough succession. If the sampling rate is too low or too high, the continuous signal may be inappropriately interpreted. As I listen to recorded music or stream a movie, I’m enjoying the fruits of optimized sampling rates.

  The concept of sampling rate has stuck with me from my undergraduate education through my medical training. When applied to healthcare, the question arises, how often do we “sample” our own health? How is this data recorded, where is it available, and who is it available to? Which signals of our bodies should be paid attention to? Is this data being sent to the local hospital? Are there engineers and software developers working with physicians and epidemiologists to scale data monitoring efforts and prevent adverse outcomes? Are these efforts integrated across institutions and communities? Sadly, some technological advances arrive at a snail’s pace to the realm of healthcare. This leaves a gap between increasing consumer demand and lagging service and product delivery. Herein lies an opportunity for venture capital to invest in companies creating a healthcare system that drives better outcomes for patients and communities through remote monitoring and data integration.

  Increasing healthcare consumer demand as a result of an aging population is a worrying scenario. There is indeed a pressing case to drastically increase the efficiency and nature of healthcare delivery, not only in the United States but globally over the next decade. As of October 2021, the World Health Organization recorded an increase in both the size and the proportion of older persons in the population for every country. By 2030, an estimated 40% increase will have occurred from 2020, in surviving persons aged 60 and over, and by 2050 this proportion will double. The US Census Bureau projects that the proportion of adults aged 65 and over will surpass the proportion of children under the age of 18 by 2035. Why does this matter? Intuitively we might correctly guess that our bodies are more prone to morbidity and mortality as we age, increasing both the complexity and likelihood of disease. Furthermore, analyzing lifetime distributions of healthcare costs clearly shows an exponential increase as we age, especially for the proportion of people aged over 65. This evidence supports the ongoing increase in the demand for healthcare product and service delivery to communities across the globe. So where do we stand in our progress towards meeting our goals? How are we improving our healthcare systems, so they can meet this increase in demand?

  The last two years through the COVID-19 Pandemic have highlighted crippling weaknesses in the healthcare infrastructure of low-, middle-, and high-income countries, with the United States as no exception. We are lagging behind comparable country averages with approximately 2 beds per 1000 persons available for acute care, hospital density decreasing at a faster rate, and consistently fewer practicing physicians for our communities. Increasing the number of beds, hospitals, and practicing physicians require capital and time-intensive endeavors across increasing hospital investments, navigating federal, state, and local regulations, increasing medical school and residency training programs, and deploying skilled practitioners to required areas. This is a gargantuan task by any measure and best left to be undertaken by appropriately equipped institutions. However, with our existing technology and capabilities, there lie opportunities in drastically increasing the efficiency and utilization of our existing healthcare infrastructure and redefining the role of hospitals and healthcare institutions in caring for the communities relying on them.

  As of 2018, the CDC reported 267.1 annual visits to physicians for every 100 persons. However, only 83.4% of people visited any healthcare professional in 2020 with the largest proportion of visits attributed to the 65 and over age group. That roughly translates to a licensed physician “sampling” 84% of the population’s health 3 times each year. The cost of inefficient utilization of healthcare systems is unfortunately measured in preventable deaths and increased disease burden on communities. An analysis of amenable deaths in 137 countries exposed a lack of appropriate utilization of health care services and low quality of healthcare systems translating to 15.6 million excess deaths each year.

  The role for venture capital catalyzing commercialization of high growth industries has long been recognized. Opportunities not only lie in investing in this growing market, but also in playing an important role in catalyzing the changing commercial healthcare landscape. Data sources are already available and being integrated but remain under-utilized. Banks and personal finance apps already have access to crucial healthcare data such as how often consumers eat out at restaurants, fast food chains, or shop for groceries. While granularity may be missing, not only is data already available to calculate individual consumption trends, but the data could be applied to models informed by studies to predict risks of adverse healthcare outcomes.

  Integrating data from remote monitoring and other sources allows for a redefined role for healthcare systems to leverage data and technological innovation toward preventative care. It expands their impact and increases the efficiency and utilization of existing healthcare infrastructure. Companies looking to capitalize on integrating data from multiple sources and creating healthcare profiles of both patients and communities stand to make an outsized impact and directly redefine and restructure the role of healthcare institutions in charge of public health outcomes. The confluence of healthcare data integration is likely to reveal trends greater than the sum of its parts in both individual and community health trajectories, informing healthcare policy and proving extremely valuable in scaling evidence-based healthcare delivery.

<p>In engineering school, I learned about sampling rates, though I was too anxious about getting through signal processing to see what they meant beyond the class. There is no such thing as a moving picture. Film is 24 still frames a second, shown fast enough to fool the eye; a flip book is the same trick, drawn by hand. Sampling records a continuous signal at separate points in time. Sample often enough and the continuous signal can be recreated, up to a <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nyquist_frequency">limit</a>. Sample too slowly and the signal is misread, a distortion called <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aliasing">aliasing</a>. Every song I stream and every movie I watch runs on well-chosen sampling rates.</p>

<p>The idea stuck with me from my undergraduate education through my medical training. Applied to healthcare, it raises a question: how often do we sample our own health? How is that data recorded, where does it live, and who can see it? Which signals from our bodies deserve attention? Does the data reach the local hospital? Are engineers and software developers working with physicians and epidemiologists to scale monitoring and prevent adverse outcomes, and are those efforts connected across institutions and communities? Some technologies arrive in healthcare at a snail’s pace, leaving a gap between rising demand for care and lagging services and products. Herein lies an opportunity: to build a healthcare system that improves outcomes for patients and communities through more frequent <span class="claim" data-ref="malasinghe2019">remote monitoring</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-malasinghe2019">1</a></sup> and the integration of signals that allow proactive intervention.</p>

<p>Today, outside of annual visits, patients mostly see their providers once they are already unwell. <span class="claim" data-ref="cdc">In 2018, the CDC counted 267.1 visits to physician offices for every 100 people, and in 2020 only 83.4 percent of people saw any health professional at all</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-cdc">2</a></sup>, with the largest share of visits made by people 65 and over. Roughly, then, physicians sample the health of four in five people about three times a year. That is an abysmal sampling rate at which to appreciate a signal.</p>

<p>A low sampling rate does not just miss detail; it can misread the signal entirely. Blood pressure is the clearest case. A reading taken in clinic can look normal while pressure across a whole day runs high, or look high only because the patient is in a clinic. <span class="claim" data-ref="banegas2018">In nearly 64,000 primary care patients in Spain, 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure predicted death more strongly than clinic readings, and masked hypertension, normal in clinic but high over the day, carried a greater risk of death than sustained hypertension</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-banegas2018">3</a></sup>. Intermittent rhythms such as paroxysmal atrial fibrillation can come and go entirely between visits. Sampling far more often is already possible: <span class="claim" data-ref="perez2019">in the Apple Heart Study, a smartwatch monitored 419,297 people for a median of 117 days and flagged an irregular pulse in 0.52 percent of them, and 84 percent of its notifications matched atrial fibrillation on a simultaneous ECG</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-perez2019">4</a></sup>.</p>

<p>Meanwhile, demand for care is rising with an aging population, in the United States and worldwide. <span class="claim" data-ref="who2025">Every country is seeing growth in both the size and the proportion of its older population. By 2030, the number of people aged 60 and over will grow by 40 percent from 2020, to 1.4 billion, and by 2050 their number will double, to 2.1 billion</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-who2025">5</a></sup>. <span class="claim" data-ref="vespa2018">In the United States, adults 65 and over are projected to outnumber children under 18 by 2034</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-vespa2018">6</a></sup>. Our bodies are more prone to illness as we age, and disease becomes both more likely and more complex. Health care costs rise slowly through adult life and climb exponentially after age 50; <span class="claim" data-ref="alemayehu2004">nearly half of a lifetime’s health care spending falls in the senior years</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-alemayehu2004">7</a></sup>.</p>

<p>The COVID-19 pandemic exposed crippling weaknesses in the healthcare infrastructure of low-, middle-, and high-income countries, and the United States was no exception. <span class="claim" data-ref="shanosky2020">The United States had 2.5 acute care beds per 1,000 people in 2017, against a comparable-country average of 4.2; its hospital density is falling faster, and it has fewer practicing physicians per person than most comparable countries</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-shanosky2020">8</a></sup>. Adding beds, hospitals, and physicians takes capital, regulatory approval at every level of government, more medical school and residency positions, and years of training: slow and expensive by any measure. With the technology we already have, however, we can use our existing infrastructure far more efficiently and redefine how hospitals care for the communities that rely on them.</p>

<p>The cost of using health systems poorly is counted in preventable deaths and added disease. <span class="claim" data-ref="kruk2018">In 137 low- and middle-income countries in 2016, 8.6 million deaths were amenable to health care: 5.0 million among people who received poor-quality care and 3.6 million among people who did not use care at all</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-kruk2018">9</a></sup>.</p>

<p>Useful data streams also lie under unturned stones. Banks and personal finance apps already hold data that bear on health, such as how often people eat out, buy fast food, or shop for groceries. The data are coarse, but they could feed risk models built on studies such as one that <span class="claim" data-ref="sun2019">linked frequent fried food with higher all-cause and cardiovascular mortality</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-sun2019">10</a></sup>. None of it works without consent: people should choose what is shared, with whom, and for what.</p>

<p>Sampling faster is not free. In signal processing, a higher sampling rate only costs storage; in medicine, every extra measurement is another chance for noise, a false alarm, or a finding that leads to tests and treatment no one needed. <span class="claim" data-ref="perez2019">In the Apple Heart Study, only 34 percent of notified participants who later wore an ECG patch showed atrial fibrillation on it, and 57 percent of those who answered a follow-up survey had contacted a health care provider outside the study</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-perez2019">4</a></sup>. Still, I would bet that populations that monitor themselves at higher sampling rates will come to enjoy lower morbidity and mortality and, after a few cycles, need fewer interventions. The first generations may pay for that learning, through too much intervention in some places and too little in others, so that later generations inherit a calibrated system: which signals matter, how often to sample them, and when to act.</p>

<p>Integrating data from remote monitoring and other sources lets health systems turn toward preventive care, expanding their reach and making better use of the infrastructure they already have. Instead of a few encounters a year, often when patients are already sick, each patient would have a continuous health profile built from monitoring, consumption, and activity data. For individuals, that widens a physician’s view of their patient’s behavior and disease patterns, and may improve treatment and outcomes. For communities, aggregate trends allow public health monitoring, resources allocated by measured need, and a cycle of innovation that follows changing needs. Integrated across communities, these data are likely to reveal trends greater than the sum of their parts, informing policy and scaling evidence-based care. By backing teams that build this system of shared, integrated data between patients and providers, funders and communities can help shift healthcare from cure to prevention.</p>
