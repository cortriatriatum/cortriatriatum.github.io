  Having recently completed a phd, a few frustrating experiences across multiple years have culminated in an insight i thought was worth sharing. I believe the atomic unit of scientific information is a single experiment. And for as long as we ignore this fact and do not operate based on it, we’re often shooting ourselves in the foot.

  Too often have i run an experiment that never worked, in my personal estimation, at least a magnitude more. In fact, informal conversations of phd progress usually relate the first 3 years of experiments not working with the last year where everything works and then is scaled. Too often have i failed to replicate failed experiments due to obscure protocol details not included in the published methods or even the supplement. Not because of falsification, just lack of detail, leading to inefficiency i had to pay for in time, reagents, money, and (perhaps soon to be professionally managed) anxiety.

  I’ve just finished writing some thoughts on why peer review should be overhauled - see link. Further malignant is the misaligned moulding of scienitifc papers and narratives, with positive publication bias being a regular practice of the formal peer review process today. What goes in submission to the peer review process is rarely what comes out in publication and not always for the better of the science itself. Perhaps provenance publication, preprints, and peer review logs are critical to the process as much as the polished output is.

  Science is somehow both a stochastic and deterministic process - governing processing ranging from strictly in each domain to everything gray in between. The scientific method is a torch, lamp, or light in the dark, it lights the way ahead, and hopefully we’re all agreed that forward is the way we’re all travelling.

  However, when we fail to share oru failures, we shoot ourselves and everyone behind us in the foot and doom ourselves to spirals of inefficiency which are now scaling with ai tools. When ai tools - llms, and other machine learning algos - run on the biased, published, scientific corpus, they exemplify our biases, what we choose to include, and remain blind to what we choose to leave out.

  In the scientific method, every result, whether negative, inconclusive, or positive informs the next experiment. We have largely treated ourselves as the only one who needs to learn from our failures when we needed to be sharing any and all results with everyone. A negative experiment points a different way, an inconclusive one drives better design. And it is the robustness and strength of the design that ultimately decides how well the path is lit, not the significant figures of a p value and its magnitude below the 0.05 threshold.

  Rewarding robust design and incentivizing experimental publication may be an antidote to combating the publication bias and the mangled mess that is the publuished and peer reviewed scientific corpus today. As we begin to scale hypothesis generation and data analyses, it is critical we do not scale our ineffiencies lest we go further off course in our path forward.

<p>Having recently completed my PhD, I came away from a few frustrating experiences across those years with an insight I think is worth sharing: the atomic unit of scientific information is a single experiment. As long as we do not operate on that fact, we are shooting ourselves, and the growing base of future knowledge, in the foot.</p>

<p>Too often, I ran an experiment that did not work. By my own estimate, the experiments that failed outnumbered those that worked, or that fit my original research program, by at least an order of magnitude. Informal conversations about PhD progress tell the same story: three years of experiments that do not work, then a final year in which everything works and is rushed to the finish. Too often I failed to replicate published experiments because of obscure protocol details left out of the methods and even the supplement. Not because of falsification, just a lack of detail, and I paid for the inefficiency in time, reagents, money, and (perhaps soon to be professionally managed) anxiety. I am not alone. <span class="claim" data-ref="errington2021">When one project set out to repeat 193 experiments from high-impact cancer biology papers, not one was described in enough detail to repeat without asking the original authors</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-errington2021">1</a></sup>.</p>

<p>I recently wrote about why <a href="/me/essays/peer-review-scaled-and-continuous/">peer review should be overhauled</a>. An even more malignant facet of the current system is the molding of papers into tidy narratives, with positive publication bias a routine part of formal peer review. <span class="claim" data-ref="franco2014">Studies with strong results are 40 percentage points more likely to be published than studies with null results, mostly because the null results are never written up</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-franco2014">2</a></sup>. <span class="claim" data-ref="dwan2008">By favoring positive results over robust design, we have inherited a literature short on negative and inconclusive results</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-dwan2008">3</a></sup>. What goes into peer review is rarely what comes out, and not always for the better: <span class="claim" data-ref="dwan2013">when published trials are compared with their own protocols, 40 to 62 percent have at least one primary outcome that was changed, introduced, or omitted</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-dwan2013">4</a></sup>. Provenance, <a href="https://www.biorxiv.org/">preprints</a>, and <a href="https://elifesciences.org/about/peer-review">peer review logs</a> may be as critical as the polished output, which is why every essay on this site shows its original draft, its edits, and its sources.</p>

<p>Science is somehow both stochastic and deterministic, investigating processes that range from strictly one or the other to every shade of gray in between. The scientific method is a light in the dark that reveals the path ahead, and hopefully we all agree that forward is the way we should be traveling.</p>

<p>When we fail to share our failures, however, we shoot ourselves, and everyone behind us, in the foot, and doom ourselves to spirals of inefficiency that machine learning tools are now scaling. When large language models and other machine learning algorithms learn from the biased, published scientific corpus, they amplify our biases: they absorb what we choose to include and remain blind to what we leave out. My guess, from years in research labs, is that negative and inconclusive results outnumber published positive results by an order of magnitude or more, sitting unpublished and poorly collated in lab notebooks. If data today is truly as valuable as oil, we have poured a fair amount of it down the drain, and it will not be easy to retrieve.</p>

<p>In the scientific method, every result, whether negative, inconclusive, or positive, informs the next experiment. Yet we have treated ourselves as the only ones who need to learn from our failures, when we should have been sharing every result with everyone. A negative experiment points a different way; an inconclusive one drives better design. <span class="claim" data-ref="asa2016">Ultimately, it is the robustness of the design that decides how well the path is lit, not how far a p-value falls below 0.05</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-asa2016">5</a></sup>.</p>

<p>Rewarding design already works. <span class="claim" data-ref="scheel2021">When journals agree to publish a study on the strength of its design, before the results are known, the share of positive results falls from 96 percent to 44 percent</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-scheel2021">6</a></sup>. The single experiment is already publishable: <a href="https://www.micropublication.org/">microPublication Biology</a> <span class="claim" data-ref="raciti2018">publishes individual, peer-reviewed findings, including results that would otherwise be left out of a paper’s narrative</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-raciti2018">7</a></sup>. A null result from a weak design teaches little, which is exactly why we should reward design, not outcome.</p>

<p>Rewarding robust design and incentivizing experimental publication may be the antidote to publication bias and to the mangled mess that is today’s published, peer-reviewed scientific corpus. As we begin to scale hypothesis generation and data analysis, we must not scale our inefficiencies along with them, or we will drift even further off course.</p>
