Every day, treating teams encounter patients in circumstances that raise questions that are difficult to answer with strong supporting evidence. Where evidence does exist, it often ranks low on the hierarchy that ranges from high-quality randomized trials to the lower rungs of expert opinion. Across guidelines in multiple medical specialties, the majority of recommendations do not rely on the strongest types of evidence. Improving evidence quality for clinical decisions could directly impact clinical outcomes.

For clinical questions and decisions that may benefit from scientific experimentation, scientists must first be made aware of the questions within their appropriate clinical context. Yet physicians are rarely the ones conducting benchtop science and researchers are rarely the ones treating patients at the bedside.

Physician-scientists are a minority of the workforce and are resource intensive to train. Physician-scientist trainees average over eight years to obtain both an MD and a PhD. This follows a four-year undergraduate degree and precedes three to seven years of additional residency training prior to independent clinical practice. Therefore, physician-scientists are not a scalable solution in bridging benchtop science to the patient bedside. As a result, the majority of biomedical researchers are likely blind to the clinical questions and decisions made during the clinical course of their disease of interest.

Several programs already attempt to bridge this gap; however, no solution has yet done so at scale. Medical research remains blind to broad swaths of clinical questions, and a large number of clinical decisions today rely on a suboptimal quality of evidence for guidance and management.
