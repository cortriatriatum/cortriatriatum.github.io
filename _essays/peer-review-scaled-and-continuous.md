original: |
  Formal peer-review as it is practiced today is significantly flawed and should be overhauled.

  The magnitude of plagiarism, falsification, fabrication and lack of reproducibility is testament to the inability of the peer review system to independently verify the findings of scientific output as it is practiced today.

  The guardian wrote a piece about how the publication system breaks the mechanisms of capitalism in sourcing unpaid labor and turning to the exact same consumer in sourcing paid demand for their own labor.

  While services such as researchhub have gone so far as creating entire new currencies to reimagine this system, the bureaucracy of academia has bent to the exorbitant fees to access their own output as a field as well as retain professional progression to h indices, publication counts, and impact factors, none of which are true measures of true research impact.

  For peer review at least, a scaled and continuous form seems to be an obvious solution applicable today. Scientific papers through preprint servers that may be able to invite comment and annotate consensus through a verifiably informed group of peers can make scientific papers into living, breathing documents with continuous peer review sourced from a scaled and an informed crowd vs the 2-3 peer reviewers editors struggle everyday to source who may not be the best suited to the task. The label it then provides for that paper is often unjustified.

<p>Formal peer review, as practiced today, is significantly flawed. Chief among its problems: we treat the judgment of two or three people, made once, as a permanent label of quality.</p>

<p>The scale of plagiarism, falsification, fabrication, and irreproducibility shows how little that label verifies. <span class="claim" data-ref="chalmers2009">By one estimate, billions of dollars invested in medical research are avoidably wasted</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-chalmers2009">1</a></sup>, <span class="claim" data-ref="yordanov2015">driven in part by poorly designed studies and contorted incentives</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-yordanov2015">2</a></sup>. <span class="claim" data-ref="fanelli2009">About 2 percent of scientists admit to fabricating or falsifying data</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-fanelli2009">3</a></sup>, and <span class="claim" data-ref="fang2012">two-thirds of retracted papers were pulled for misconduct, not honest error</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-fang2012">4</a></sup>. <span class="claim" data-ref="osc2015">When researchers repeated 100 published psychology studies, only about a third held up</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-osc2015">5</a></sup>. <span class="claim" data-ref="schroter2008">When the <i>BMJ</i> planted nine major errors in a paper, reviewers caught fewer than three on average</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-schroter2008">6</a></sup>. Peer review was never designed to catch fraud; reviewers rarely see the raw data. That is exactly the problem: we give its stamp a weight it cannot carry.</p>

<p>The economics are strange, too. <span class="claim" data-ref="buranyi2017"><a href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/2017/jun/27/profitable-business-scientific-publishing-bad-for-science"><i>The Guardian</i></a> has described how publishers take unpaid labor from scientists, who write and review the papers, and then sell the results back to those same scientists’ institutions</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-buranyi2017">7</a></sup>. <span class="claim" data-ref="lariviere2015">Five publishers now account for more than half of all papers</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-lariviere2015">8</a></sup>, and <span class="claim" data-ref="aczel2021">researchers donated more than 100 million hours to peer review in 2020 alone</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-aczel2021">9</a></sup>. Academia pays exorbitant fees to read its own output, and it still ties careers to <span class="claim" data-ref="koltun2021">h-indices, publication counts, and impact factors, none of which truly measure research impact</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-koltun2021">10</a></sup>. Meanwhile, editors struggle every day to find two or three reviewers, who may not be the best suited to the task, and the label those reviewers confer is often unjustified.</p>

<p>For peer review, at least, a scaled and continuous alternative is workable today. Papers posted to preprint servers such as <a href="https://www.biorxiv.org/">bioRxiv</a> could invite comment and record consensus from a verifiably informed group of peers. Each paper would become a living, breathing document, reviewed continuously by a scaled and informed crowd rather than a few overworked strangers. There is early evidence this works. <span class="claim" data-ref="ritter2017">The chemistry journal <i>Synlett</i> gave a crowd of about 100 reviewers 72 hours to comment on submitted papers</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-ritter2017">11</a></sup>, and <span class="claim" data-ref="vangemmeren2020">crowd review is now its default method</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-vangemmeren2020">12</a></sup>. Readers already catch what reviewers miss: <span class="claim" data-ref="bik2016">screening published papers, one team found problematic images in 3.8 percent of more than 20,000 biomedical papers</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-bik2016">13</a></sup>.</p>

<p>Pieces of this already exist. <a href="https://pubpeer.com/">PubPeer</a> hosts post-publication critique, <a href="https://elifesciences.org/about/peer-review">eLife</a> publishes reviewed preprints with public assessments, and <a href="https://www.researchhub.com/">ResearchHub</a> has gone as far as creating its own currency to pay reviewers. What is missing is the whole: verified expertise, real incentives to take part, and a visible, evolving record of where each paper stands, with methods, statistics, and conclusions assessed separately.</p>

<p>None of this is easy. Open commenting draws little real review: <span class="claim" data-ref="malicki2021">in one analysis of <a href="https://www.biorxiv.org/">bioRxiv</a> preprints, only 12 percent of readers’ comments were full review reports</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-malicki2021">14</a></sup>. Crowds also herd: <span class="claim" data-ref="muchnik2013">in a randomized experiment, a single early up-vote inflated final ratings by 25 percent on average</span><sup class="ref"><a href="#ref-muchnik2013">15</a></sup>. A continuous system would have to verify who counts as an informed peer, reward unique and valuable input whether it challenges the crowd or confirms it, and hide the running score until each reviewer has given their own judgment. AI-generated reviews should be heavily moderated or banned; reviewers should write free-form critiques or redline the paper itself, which ensures they have read it in full. These are design problems, not reasons to keep a system whose label we already know we cannot trust.</p>

<p>A paper should not be peer reviewed once and finished. It should stay under review for as long as it is relevant to the scientific enterprise, or even longer.</p>
